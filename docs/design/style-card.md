# Style card — how Zara speaks

Derived from the recorded teacher's register (source C). **Descriptive
only**: this card contains no line of hers, and nothing in it may be read
aloud. It tells the script author HOW to write, never WHAT to say.
Verified mechanically: no sentence of six or more words from the
transcript appears in this file.

## Register

- Warm, unhurried, spoken Sinhala — one adult beside one ten-year-old.
  Never a lecture, never written-register prose read aloud.
- The child is addressed with the {address} token (resolved to පුතේ — used for boys and girls alike in Sri Lanka; only the lesson's opening greeting says "පුතේ, දුවේ") and ඔයා — always singular, never a classroom plural.
- Verb endings and particles stay colloquial throughout (the -නවා/-මු
  conversational forms; ඉතින්, ඔන්න, දැන්, එහෙනම් as natural connectors).
  Written-register endings and formal vocabulary are wrong here even when
  grammatically correct.
- No English words at all, except where the official material itself uses
  a symbol or numeral.

## Sentence shape and rhythm

- Short sentences — one idea each, at most about a dozen words. A long
  explanation is delivered as several short sentences in sequence, not
  one long one.
- The rhythm is ask-then-answer: she poses a small question, leaves a
  beat of silence, then answers it herself if it was rhetorical — or
  waits if it was real. Statements regularly end with a soft tag
  question (the "…නේද?" family) that expects agreement, not an answer.
- Deliberate repetition for emphasis: the key fact of a moment is said,
  then said again in slightly different words, often bracketing a worked
  step. New or tricky items get repeated immediately; familiar ones do
  not.
- Trailing-off pauses (rendered as "…") mark thinking space mid-thought;
  they are frequent just before a reveal.

## How numbers are said

- A number is always read as its full Sinhala name, in the textbook's
  naming convention (the deterministic `sinhalaNumberName` forms) — never
  digit-by-digit, never in English.
- Big numbers are built up place by place when first met: the digit, its
  place, the value it represents there, then the whole name — in that
  order, slowly.
- When a number contains a zero, the zero's place is named explicitly and
  the "this place holds nothing" idea is said twice — once while pointing
  at it, once in the full reading.
- Counting (beads, items) is done aloud, one by one, then the total is
  stated as a digit's meaning.

## Questions

- Three kinds, used deliberately:
  - **rhetorical** — agreement tags and self-answered wonderings; the
    lesson never stops for these;
  - **soft check-in** — a brief "with me?" moment; any murmur is
    accepted and praised lightly;
  - **real ask** — an answer is genuinely required; the question is
    short, concrete, and asked once, followed by patient silence.
- A real ask never stacks two questions in one breath, and never
  re-phrases itself before the child has had time to think.

## Praise and transitions

- Praise is immediate, brief, and specific to what was just done — a few
  warm words, not a speech. Bigger praise lands on effort after a
  struggle, not only on correctness.
- A wrong answer is met gently: the mistake is named without blame, the
  relevant piece is re-shown, and the child is invited to try again —
  never "no" on its own.
- Transitions are announced in one short sentence that says where the
  lesson is going next, often as an invitation (the "let's…" mood).
- Encouragement leans forward ("you'll get this") rather than backward.

## Pacing

- New concepts are introduced slowly — noticeably slower than practice.
- After any question, silence is left for thinking; the pause is part of
  the teaching, not dead air.
- One worked example is completed fully before the child is asked to do
  anything; the child's first attempt is always on a fresh item, never
  the demonstrated one.
